If you mean the traditional clinical approach to assessing insulin resistance (IR), clinicians usually do not diagnose it from one single test. They combine history, physical findings, glucose-related laboratory tests, and sometimes more specialized measures.

Traditional clinical assessment of insulin resistance

1. Clinical history and examination Clinicians look for features associated with insulin resistance, such as:

- Central/abdominal obesity
- Increased waist circumference or BMI
- High blood pressure
- Dyslipidemia, especially high triglycerides and low HDL
- Family history of type 2 diabetes
- Sedentary lifestyle
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Acanthosis nigricans (dark, thickened skin, commonly around the neck or armpits)

2. Fasting blood tests Commonly measured:

- Fasting plasma glucose (FPG)
- Fasting insulin
- HbA1c
- Lipid profile

Fasting insulin isn't routinely required for diagnosing diabetes, but it can be used in research or when clinicians are specifically evaluating insulin resistance.

3. HOMA-IR One of the traditional surrogate measures is HOMA-IR (Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance).

With glucose in mg/dL:

\[
\text{HOMA-IR} =
\frac{\text{Fasting insulin (µU/mL)}\times\text{Fasting glucose (mg/dL)}}{405}
\]

With glucose in mmol/L:

\[
\text{HOMA-IR} =
\frac{\text{Fasting insulin (µU/mL)}\times\text{Fasting glucose (mmol/L)}}{22.5}
\]

A higher HOMA-IR generally indicates greater insulin resistance, but there isn't one universally accepted cutoff because results depend on the population, insulin assay, and laboratory.

4. Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) The traditional OGTT measures glucose response after consuming a standardized glucose drink. Glucose is usually measured fasting and then at specified intervals, commonly at 2 hours.

Some clinicians/researchers also measure insulin during an OGTT to examine the insulin response, but insulin measurements aren't standardized enough to be a universal clinical diagnostic test for IR.

5. More direct research methods The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is generally considered the reference method for directly measuring insulin sensitivity. It is highly informative but technically demanding, expensive, and therefore not routinely used in ordinary clinical practice.

In practical clinical medicine

A typical evaluation might therefore look like:

History + physical examination → fasting glucose/HbA1c + lipid profile → consider fasting insulin/HOMA-IR or OGTT when appropriate → investigate associated conditions.

One important point: insulin resistance, prediabetes, and diabetes are not the same thing. A person can have substantial insulin resistance while their fasting glucose is still normal because the pancreas compensates by producing more insulin.
